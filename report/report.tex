{\large Final Project, Scenario 2 \;|\; Data Analytics and Statistics with Applications (LEAP)}\\[0.4ex]

\noindent\textbf{Research question.} Which student characteristics are associated with final Mathematics performance, which factors are linked to the risk of failing, and what conclusions can reasonably be drawn from the data?

\noindent\textbf{Summary.} Previous class failures is the strongest and most robust correlate of a low final grade and of failing. Going out with friends more often and lower maternal education are also associated with lower grades. All models explain only about a fifth of the variation in grades. A key finding of the data checks is that 38 students recorded with a final grade of 0 almost certainly did not sit the final assessment; they distort the apparent role of absences and inflate some coefficients. Because the data are observational, none of these associations can be read as causal effects.

%==============================================================================
\section{Understanding and preparing the data}

The data are the Mathematics file of the UCI Student Performance dataset (Cortez, 2008), collected in 2005 to 2006 from two Portuguese secondary schools, Gabriel Pereira (349 students) and Mousinho da Silveira (46 students), through school reports and a student questionnaire. \textbf{One row is one student} (395 rows, 33 variables).

\textbf{Outcomes.} The main outcome is the final grade \texttt{G3} (0 to 20). The second outcome is a pass/fail indicator created from it (Pass if $\texttt{G3} \geq 10$). The earlier-period grades \texttt{G1} and \texttt{G2} are kept in the data but are not used as predictors, as required by the brief.

\textbf{Variable types.} \emph{Numerical:} age (15 to 22), absences (0 to 75), and failures (a count top-coded at 3, meaning ``3 or more''). \emph{Ordered categories coded as numbers:} mother's and father's education (\texttt{Medu}, \texttt{Fedu}, 0 to 4), travel time and weekly study time (1 to 4), and family relations, free time, going out, workday and weekend alcohol use and health (1 to 5). \emph{Nominal or binary categories:} school, sex, address, family size, parents' cohabitation, parents' jobs, reason for school choice, guardian, and yes/no items such as extra school support, paid classes, wish to pursue higher education, internet access and romantic relationship. Nominal variables were converted to factors in R. Ordered variables were kept as integer scores; treating, for example, the step from ``2 to 5 hours'' to ``5 to 10 hours'' of study as equal in size to the step below it is a simplification I return to in Section~5.

\textbf{Data quality.} There are no missing values and no duplicated rows. The one clearly problematic feature is that \textbf{38 students (9.6\%) have $\texttt{G3}=0$}, a gap below which no student scores between 1 and 3. All 38 have exactly zero recorded absences (against 22\% of other students), they had non-zero first-period grades (mean \texttt{G1} = 7.5) and 13 of them already had $\texttt{G2}=0$. The most plausible reading is that these students did not sit the final assessment (dropped out or stopped attending) and that absences were not recorded for them, rather than that they sat the exam and scored zero. I keep them in the main analysis, since not completing the course is a genuine fail, but test in Section~5 how far the conclusions depend on them.

\textbf{Responsible use.} Although anonymised, the data contain sensitive items about minors: alcohol use, romantic relationships, family structure and parents' jobs. In schools of this size, a combination of school, age, sex and parents' jobs could identify individuals. Results of this kind can also be misused to label or profile students, or to justify treating groups (for example by sex or family background) differently. Any use should be aimed at offering support rather than sanctions, and conclusions should be phrased as group-level associations, never as predictions about named individuals.

\section{Exploring the data}

The final grade has a mean of 10.4 and a median of 11 (SD 4.6, IQR 8 to 14). Without the zero group, the distribution is roughly symmetric around 11 (Figure~\ref{fig:dist}). \textbf{67.1\% of students pass} (265) and 32.9\% fail (130); 38 of the 130 fails are the zero-grade students. Absences are strongly right-skewed (median 4, mean 5.7, maximum 75), with a few extreme values. Most students have no previous failures (312 of 395; 50 have one, 17 two, 16 three or more). Sex is balanced (208 female, 187 male), while the wish to pursue higher education is nearly universal (375 yes, 20 no), so any comparison involving the ``no'' group rests on very few students.

Group comparisons point to clear differences. The mean final grade falls from 11.3 for students with no previous failures to 8.1, 6.2 and 5.7 for one, two and three or more failures (Figure~\ref{fig:groups}a). Students who report going out ``low'' (2 on the scale) have the highest mean grade (11.2), and those who go out ``very high'' the lowest (9.0); the pattern is not strictly linear, as the ``very low'' group also scores below average (Figure~\ref{fig:groups}b). Male students have a slightly higher mean grade than female students (10.9 vs 10.0) and a higher pass rate (70.6\% vs 63.9\%).

\section{Relationships between variables}

\textbf{Correlations.} Because most candidate predictors are ordered codes, I computed Spearman (rank) correlations alongside Pearson; the two give the same ranking (full heatmap in Appendix~A). The strongest association with \texttt{G3} is previous failures ($r = -0.36$), followed by mother's education ($r = 0.22$), age ($-0.16$), father's education ($0.15$) and going out ($-0.13$; Spearman $-0.17$). Study time is only weakly related ($r = 0.10$), and absences show essentially no linear relationship ($r = 0.03$). Two pairs of predictors overlap strongly: \texttt{Medu} and \texttt{Fedu} ($r = 0.62$) and workday and weekend alcohol use ($r = 0.65$), so only one of each pair is a sensible model candidate. Age is partly a proxy for failures ($r = 0.24$), since students who repeat a year are older.

\textbf{Contingency tables.} Figure~\ref{fig:pass} reports column percentages. Students with at least one previous failure pass at half the rate of those with none (37.3\% vs 75.0\%; $\chi^2 = 40$, $p < 0.001$). Students who do not want to go on to higher education pass at 35\% against 69\% (Fisher's exact test $p = 0.003$), but this rests on only 20 students. Students receiving extra school support pass \emph{less} often (54.9\% vs 68.9\%, $p = 0.07$). This should not be read as support harming results: support is targeted at weaker students, so the association reflects selection (reverse causation). For that reason \texttt{schoolsup} is not used as a predictor.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig5_pass_rates.png}
\caption{Pass rate (\% with $\texttt{G3}\geq 10$) within each group, with group sizes.}
\label{fig:pass}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why absences look unrelated.} Figure~\ref{fig:abs} shows that the 38 zero-grade students, all with zero absences, sit at the bottom left of the scatterplot. Among the remaining 357 students, the fitted line slopes downward. The near-zero overall correlation is therefore an artefact of how the zero group was recorded, not evidence that absences do not matter.

\textbf{Variables chosen for modelling.} Previous failures, mother's education, going out and sex are the most strongly related characteristics. I add study time and absences because they are behaviours of direct interest to a school, and the wish for higher education because of its large pass-rate gap. The resulting set of seven predictors covers academic history, family background, behaviour and motivation without including strongly overlapping pairs.

\section{Linear and logistic regression models}

\textbf{Multiple linear regression.} The main model is
$\texttt{G3} \sim \texttt{failures} + \texttt{Medu} + \texttt{goout} + \texttt{studytime} + \texttt{absences} + \texttt{sex} + \texttt{higher}$,
with female and ``does not want higher education'' as the reference categories (Table~\ref{tab:lin}).

\emph{Interpretation.} Holding the other predictors constant, each additional previous failure is associated with a final grade about \textbf{1.8 points lower} on the 20-point scale. For a typical student (mother with secondary education, average going out, 2 to 5 hours of study, 4 absences, female, wants higher education), the predicted grade is 10.5 with no previous failures and 6.9 with two. Each additional level of mother's education is associated with a grade about 0.5 points higher, and each step up the going-out scale with a grade about 0.5 points lower, again holding the other predictors constant. Male students are predicted to score about 1.4 points higher than female students with the same values on the other predictors.

The model explains 18.6\% of the variation in final grades ($R^2$). Adjusted $R^2$ rises from 0.153 for the starter model to 0.171, and AIC falls (2266 to 2259), so the two added predictors improve the fit modestly. Still, more than 80\% of the variation is left unexplained, and a typical prediction error of about 4 grade points is large on a 20-point scale.

\textbf{Logistic regression.} The same predictors are used to model the probability of passing. Table~\ref{tab:log} reports odds ratios (OR); an OR below 1 means lower odds of passing, holding the other predictors constant.

\emph{Interpretation.} Each additional previous failure multiplies the odds of passing by about 0.42, that is, it reduces the odds by roughly 58\%, holding the other predictors constant. Each step up the going-out scale multiplies the odds by about 0.71. Because an odds ratio is not a change in probability, it helps to translate: for the typical student described above, the predicted probability of passing is \textbf{72\% with no previous failures and 31\% with two}. With no failures, it is 84\% if she goes out ``very little'' and 56\% if she goes out ``very much'' (Appendix~B). Male students have 1.74 times the odds of passing of comparable female students, though the interval is wide.

\emph{What each model adds.} The linear model describes how far grades differ across the whole range, while the logistic model addresses the decision that matters most for a school, the risk of failing, and lets results be expressed as probabilities. Notably, mother's education is associated with grade level but not clearly with the chance of passing. As a classifier, however, the logistic model is weak: using a 0.5 cut-off it classifies 72.9\% of students correctly, only modestly above the 67.1\% achieved by predicting ``Pass'' for everyone, and it identifies just 35\% of the students who actually failed (45 of 130).

\section{Evaluating the analysis}

\textbf{Residuals.} The residuals-versus-fitted plot (Figure~\ref{fig:resid}a) has one clear warning sign: the 38 zero-grade students form a straight diagonal band of large negative residuals (residual $= -$fitted value), well separated from the rest. For the other students the smoothed line stays close to zero and the spread is fairly even, apart from upward curvature at the lowest fitted values, where there are few observations. The outcome is also bounded (0 to 20) and takes only whole-number values, which a linear model does not respect. The Q-Q plot (Appendix~A) shows a heavy left tail produced by the same group.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{subfigure}{0.48\textwidth}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig8_residuals_vs_fitted.png}\caption{Residuals vs fitted, main linear model}\end{subfigure}\hfill
\begin{subfigure}{0.48\textwidth}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig6_absences_vs_g3.png}\caption{Absences vs final grade}\label{fig:abs}\end{subfigure}
\caption{Model diagnostics and the role of the zero-grade group (red).}
\label{fig:resid}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Uncertainty.} In the linear model, previous failures is the most convincing result: the 95\% CI of $-2.41$ to $-1.20$ is far from zero ($t = -5.85$). Mother's education and going out are also statistically significant at the 5\% level, but their intervals come close to zero (0.09 to 0.89 and $-0.83$ to $-0.08$), so the true associations could be very small. The higher-education coefficient is large (1.8 points) but uncertain (CI $-0.24$ to 3.80, $p = 0.08$) because only 20 students answered ``no''. In the logistic model, the OR for failures (0.29 to 0.59) and for going out (0.57 to 0.87) both exclude 1; the OR for sex (1.06 to 2.89) only just does, and the OR for higher education (0.71 to 6.62) is too imprecise to support any conclusion.

\textbf{Statistical significance and practical importance.} The two do not always coincide. A 1.8-point difference per failure is practically large: it can move a student from a pass to a fail. A 0.5-point difference per level of going out or of maternal education is statistically detectable but small relative to a residual SD of 4.2 points. Conversely, the higher-education gap may be practically important even though it is not statistically convincing here.

\textbf{Overlap between predictors.} Variance inflation factors range from 1.03 to 1.19, and no pair of model predictors correlates above 0.24 in absolute value, so multicollinearity is not a concern for this specification. Overlap was avoided by design by excluding \texttt{Fedu} and the alcohol variables.

\textbf{Sensitivity analysis.} Re-fitting both models without the 38 zero-grade students changes several conclusions (Appendix~C):
\begin{itemize}
\item The association with \textbf{absences reverses sign}: each extra absence is now associated with a grade 0.07 points lower (CI $-0.10$ to $-0.03$, $p < 0.001$) and an OR for passing of 0.95.
\item The failures coefficient roughly \textbf{halves} ($-0.98$, CI $-1.47$ to $-0.48$) but remains clearly significant. Part of its original size reflects the fact that zero-grade students had more previous failures (mean 0.92 vs 0.27).
\item Study time becomes significant (0.42, $p = 0.04$), while the higher-education coefficient shrinks to almost zero and the sex difference in passing is no longer significant.
\item Mother's education and going out are almost unchanged, so these associations are robust.
\end{itemize}
The conclusions about failures, going out and maternal education therefore hold in both versions; those about absences, study time, sex and higher education depend on how the non-completers are treated and should be stated cautiously.

\textbf{Other limitations.} Ordered predictors are entered as linear scores, but the going-out pattern is visibly non-linear, so a single slope is a simplification. The predictor set was chosen after looking at the same data, so $p$-values are somewhat optimistic. No out-of-sample validation was performed.

\section{Causality, limitations and conclusions}

\textbf{Observational data.} The data come from school records and a questionnaire completed at one point in time; nothing was assigned or manipulated. The regression coefficients therefore describe associations, not effects. We cannot conclude, for example, that reducing going out would raise grades.

\textbf{Confounding and reverse causation.} Important influences are not measured: prior mathematical ability, motivation, teacher and class, household income and peer effects. Previous failures is best understood as a marker of earlier difficulties rather than a cause, and it is plausibly driven by the same unmeasured ability that drives \texttt{G3}. Some relationships may run in the opposite direction: students who struggle may disengage socially in different ways, and extra support is given \emph{because} students struggle.

\textbf{Self-reported and coarse measures.} Study time, going out, alcohol use and family relations are self-reported by teenagers on short scales and may be affected by recall and social-desirability bias. Absences come from school records but appear not to have been recorded for students who stopped attending. These measurement issues weaken and may bias the observed associations.

\textbf{Generalisation.} The sample covers two public schools in one region of Portugal, with 88\% of students from one school, in a single year (2005 to 2006), and only the Mathematics course. The findings need not apply to other schools, countries, subjects or the present day, where grading scales, repetition policies and support systems differ.

\textbf{Conclusions supported by the evidence.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Students with previous class failures have markedly lower final grades and a much higher risk of failing; this is the strongest and most robust pattern in the data.
\item More frequent going out with friends and lower maternal education are associated with modestly lower grades, consistently across specifications.
\item About one in ten students did not complete the final assessment; how these students are handled materially changes several results.
\item The measured characteristics explain only about 19\% of the variation in grades; most of what determines performance is not captured here.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Claims that would go beyond the evidence.} That failing a class, going out, or a mother's education \emph{causes} lower grades; that boys are inherently better at mathematics; that extra school support is ineffective; that absences do not matter; or that the model can reliably identify individual students who will fail.
